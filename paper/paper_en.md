::: {custom-style="Abstract"}
**Abstract.** Scorers that pool token-pair similarities with a maximum underlie late-interaction retrieval, token matching and alignment-based inference. Over context-independent token states such a scorer sees a context as a bag of tokens, cannot lower a score when tokens are added, and separates a decisive negation from an irrelevant one only through a position-aware mask. Two diagnostics turn these statements into exact item-level predictions: NegPairs-Perm, «data.perm.scenarios» permutation minimal pairs in which a decisive and an irrelevant negation contain exactly the same words, and the «nevir.nested» NevIR instances whose documents are nested as bags of tokens. A trained implementation reproduced the predictions without exception. Scored as shipped, «pub.n_checkpoints» released systems behave near that limit: every retrieval scorer (late interaction, dense, learned sparse, rerankers, monoT5) separated the permutation pairs by at most «pub.rng.retrieval.SS_bal.max» points of order-balanced scope sensitivity, the four pure late-interaction systems by at most «pub.rng.li.SS_bal.max», whereas pointwise judgments by instruction-tuned language models of 4 to 8 billion parameters reached «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.min» to «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.max». Fine-tuning two late-interaction checkpoints on standard minimal pairs made them react to negation but not to its scope («ft.rng.np.SS_bal.min» to «ft.rng.np.SS_bal.max» points) at a cost of «ft.rng.np.scifact.drop.min» to «ft.rng.np.scifact.drop.max» SciFact nDCG@10 points. A prespecified study with seven frozen encoders locates what frozen contextual states contribute: clause information is linearly present, a maximum trained on standard pairs uses a fraction of it, mostly when the decisive clause comes first, and a mask built from the decision separates the pairs where no unmasked rule can.

# 1. Introduction

A negation changes meaning within a scope. The contexts *the storage room is sufficient for extra chairs, but the main hall is insufficient for the whole team* and *the storage room is insufficient for extra chairs, but the main hall is sufficient for the whole team* contain exactly the same words, yet only the first bears on where a team should gather (Table {{ref:tab:exampleperm}}, Appendix A). Late-interaction retrieval sums, over query tokens, the maximum similarity over document tokens [@khattab2020; @santhanam2022]; BERTScore matches every token to its most similar token in the other text [@zhang2020]; alignment-based inference models pool compared token pairs [@parikh2016; @chen2017]. We call these MaxSim-family scorers: token-pair scores pooled with a maximum (or another symmetric aggregation) over the tokens of one text and combined over those of the other. Negation is known to be hard for retrieval and for pretrained encoders alike [@weller2024; @kassner2020; @ettinger2020; @hossain2022], but the known results mix two questions: what the aggregation permits, and what the encoder provides.

The first question has exact answers. Over context-independent (static) token states a symmetric aggregation sees the context as a bag of tokens (Proposition A), a monotone aggregation cannot lower any score when tokens are added (Proposition B), and a signed maximum whose mask follows the negation scope can separate what the bag cannot (Proposition C). They are elementary consequences of symmetric and monotone set functions [@zaheer2017], but they fix the outcome on specific test items for every parameter value, training set and seed. Two diagnostics make them testable: NegPairs-Perm, «data.perm.scenarios» permutation minimal pairs in which the same negation edit is applied once in the decisive clause (arm N, after which the other answer is correct) and once in the irrelevant clause (arm S, after which the answer is unchanged), so that N and S contain exactly the same words; and the nested subset of NevIR [@weller2024], the «nevir.nested» test instances whose two documents are nested as bags of tokens, which every static monotone scorer must fail. A trained implementation reproduced both predictions exactly («n.exact_checks» float64 checks, «R5.violations_total» violations).

The second question is empirical. We score «pub.n_checkpoints» released systems as they are shipped (late interaction, dense, learned sparse, cross-encoder rerankers, monoT5, and instruction-tuned language models as pointwise judges): no retrieval scorer exceeded «pub.rng.retrieval.SS_bal.max» points of order-balanced scope sensitivity on the permutation pairs, the four pure late-interaction systems had intervals that contain zero, and the judges reached «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.min» to «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.max» on the same items. Fine-tuning two late-interaction checkpoints on standard minimal pairs taught them to change their decision under a negation but not to tell where it applies, at a cost to standard retrieval. A prespecified study (design, gate and rules frozen before any new data existed; seven frozen encoders, ten seeds, «train.n_runs» runs) then asks what frozen contextual token states contribute to a maximum trained on standard pairs.

The contributions are (i) three propositions with proofs and two diagnostics that turn them into exact checks; (ii) NegPairs-Perm and NegPairs-Fresh, model-generated minimal-pair sets verified by two model families, audited, and checked by two human annotators on all permutation scenarios; (iii) a comparison of «pub.n_checkpoints» released systems on the diagnostics, with a precision control that removed «pub.prec.ties_prev_total» tied comparisons caused by bfloat16 logits; (iv) a fine-tuning experiment that separates sensitivity to negation from sensitivity to its scope; and (v) the prespecified frozen-encoder study with every gate and rule outcome reported, met or not.

# 2. Related work

**Late interaction and token matching.** ColBERT scores a query against a document by summing, over query token embeddings, the maximum cosine over document token embeddings [@khattab2020]; ColBERTv2 keeps this scoring with a compressed index [@santhanam2022], and its matching relies on exact matches for important terms [@formal2021]. BERTScore uses greedy maximum matching between contextual token embeddings [@zhang2020]; alignment-based inference models pool compared token vectors [@parikh2016; @chen2017]. All are instances of the setting of Section 3 when the token states are fixed; the propositions say nothing about the contextual encoders these systems use.

**Negation in evaluation.** Pretrained masked language models predict similar fillers for negated and affirmative probes [@kassner2020; @ettinger2020]; inference corpora contain few negations that matter for the label [@hossain2020; @hossain2022]. Dedicated resources build negation-focused contrasts: CondaQA pairs passages with paraphrase, scope and affirmative edits [@ravichander2022]; ScoNe probes scope in inference [@she2023]; NaN-NLI targets sub-clausal negation [@truong2022]; synthetic benchmarks test language models on negated sentences [@garciaferrero2023; @truong2023]; NevIR does the same for retrieval, with human-written queries over CondaQA passages [@weller2024]. These resources evaluate trained systems; we ask what one architectural choice permits, build items whose answer the propositions fix in advance, and then measure released systems on them.

**Cue, scope, word order.** Annotated corpora define negation cues and scopes [@vincze2008; @morante2012], and scope resolvers range from feature-based systems [@morante2009] to neural models [@fancellu2016; @khandelwal2020]; the signed rules studied here take a polarity mask from outside the scorer. Inference models trained on standard data largely keep their predictions under word-order permutations [@sinha2021; @pham2021]; permutation minimal pairs invert this observation, because their words are identical and their answers differ. Sum and max pooling are permutation-invariant set functions [@zaheer2017; @ilse2018].

**Controlled and prespecified evaluation.** Minimal pairs isolate a single contrast [@warstadt2020]; contrast sets and counterfactual edits probe local decision boundaries [@gardner2020; @kaushik2020]; behavioral suites organize probes by capability [@ribeiro2020]; partial-input baselines reveal artifacts [@gururangan2018; @poliak2018], and models can succeed through heuristics that fail on targeted cases [@mccoy2019]. Model-assisted data construction usually keeps human review [@liu2022wanli]. Preregistration [@vanmiltenburg2021] and care in significance testing [@dror2018] restrict analytic flexibility. Probing classifiers locate information in representations, with known pitfalls [@belinkov2022]; control tasks make a probe's selectivity measurable [@hewitt2019].

# 3. Setting and propositions

## 3.1 Setting

A context is a token sequence $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ over a vocabulary $V$ (in NegPairs the jointly encoded context and question; in NevIR a document) and an option is a sequence $o = (y_1, \dots, y_m)$ (in NevIR a query). An encoder gives token states $h_i(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$; option states $u_j(o)$ depend on the option alone. The encoder is *static* if there is a map $\varphi: V \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with $h_i(x) = \varphi(x_i)$ for every $x$ and $i$, otherwise *contextual*. Each position may carry a mask bit $\mu_i(x) \in \{0, 1\}$. A pair scorer gives $c_{ij} = g(h_i, u_j)$ for a fixed trained function $g$. For each option token $j$, an aggregator $A$ maps the multiset

$$Z_j(x, o) = \{\!\{(h_i(x), \mu_i(x)) : i = 1, \dots, n\}\!\} \qquad ({{def:eq:multiset}})$$

to a number $a_j$; $A$ may use $u_j$ and the parameters, but it reads the context positions only through this multiset. The option score is $S(o; x) = \Psi(a_1, \dots, a_m)$ for a fixed $\Psi$ (the mean over option tokens in our trained scorers, the sum in ColBERT); the prediction is the option with the higher score. $\mathrm{Bag}(x)$ is the multiset of tokens of $x$ and $\mathrm{Bag}_\mu(x)$ the multiset of (token, mask) pairs. The statements hold in exact arithmetic (rounding: Appendix B.5).

## 3.2 Aggregation rules

The trained scorers of Section 7 share the pair scorer $c_{ij} = e_{ij} + \lambda \tanh(a_{ij})$, where $e_{ij}$ is a scaled dot product of projected states, $a_{ij}$ the output of an affinity network on $[h_i; u_j; h_i - u_j; h_i \odot u_j]$ and $\lambda = \sigma(\theta)$ is learned (Appendix B.2). The maximum (A1), the mean (A2) and the log-sum-exp with a learned temperature (A3) are unmasked. The signed rules subtract the best match inside a polarity set $\mathcal{N}$ of context tokens from the best match outside it,

$$A_{\pm}\left(\{c_{ij}\}_{i};\, \mathcal{N}\right) = \max_{i \notin \mathcal{N}} c_{ij} \;-\; \beta \max_{i \in \mathcal{N}} c_{ij}, \qquad ({{def:eq:signed}})$$

with $\beta = \mathrm{softplus}(b)$ learned and an empty maximum read as zero. A4 takes $\mathcal{N}$ from a deterministic dependency-based scope resolver, A6 from a sentence-wide mask (every token of a sentence that contains a detected cue), and D1 from the data set itself (the decisive words and the cue in N items, the cue alone in S items, nothing in O and P); D1 reads the arm identity and is a ceiling, not a method. A5 replaces the hard set by a gate $p_i = \sigma(w^{\top} h_i + b_0)$ learned from the token state, with $\max_i (1 - p_i) c_{ij} - \beta \max_i p_i c_{ij}$. Two further scorers remove the affinity network: the learned MaxSim head M1 with $c_{ij} = (W h_i)^{\top}(W u_j)/\sqrt{«cfg.proj»}$, and the zero-shot cosine M0 on unit-length states, which has no parameters; M0-native is ColBERTv2's own scoring.

## 3.3 Propositions

**Proposition A (bag invariance).** If the encoder is static, then for every option $o$ the score $S(o; x)$ depends on $x$ only through $\mathrm{Bag}_\mu(x)$; for an unmasked rule it depends only on $\mathrm{Bag}(x)$.

**Proof.** For a static encoder $h_i(x) = \varphi(x_i)$, so $Z_j(x, o)$ in Eq. ({{ref:eq:multiset}}) is the image of $\mathrm{Bag}_\mu(x)$ under $(v, m) \mapsto (\varphi(v), m)$. Equal masked bags give the same multiset $Z_j$ for every $j$, hence the same $a_j$ and the same $S(o; x)$; without a mask, $\mathrm{Bag}_\mu(x)$ is $\mathrm{Bag}(x)$. $\square$

**Corollary A.1 (permutation pairs).** Let the encoder be static and the rule unmasked. If $\mathrm{Bag}(x^N) = \mathrm{Bag}(x^S)$, every option receives the same score under both contexts, so the predicted option is the same in N and S. For permutation minimal pairs whose gold answers differ it follows, for every parameter value, training set and seed, that the decision changes from O to N and from O to S coincide in every scenario (the prediction for O is the same reference in both comparisons), so the permutation scope sensitivity of Section 4.1 is 0, and that exactly one of N and S is answered correctly, so the joint (N, S) accuracy is 0.

**Corollary A.2 (masks that ignore position).** If $\mu_i(x)$ depends only on the token $x_i$ (a cue lexicon), then $\mathrm{Bag}_\mu(x)$ is a function of $\mathrm{Bag}(x)$ and Corollary A.1 holds for the masked rule: marking a cue without its scope cannot separate permutation pairs; the same holds for a sentence-level mask whenever the marked tokens of N and S form the same multiset (Corollary A.3).

**Proposition B (inclusion monotonicity).** Let the encoder be static, the rule unmasked, $A$ monotone under multiset inclusion and $\Psi$ nondecreasing in each argument. If $\mathrm{Bag}(x) \subseteq \mathrm{Bag}(x')$ (multiplicities compared), then $S(o; x) \le S(o; x')$ for every option $o$.

**Proof.** $\mathrm{Bag}(x) \subseteq \mathrm{Bag}(x')$ implies $Z_j(x, o) \subseteq Z_j(x', o)$ for every $j$; monotonicity of $A$ gives $a_j(x) \le a_j(x')$ and monotonicity of $\Psi$ gives $S(o; x) \le S(o; x')$. $\square$ The maximum (A1, M0, M1) and the log-sum-exp (A3) are monotone (an added element cannot lower a maximum and adds a positive term inside the logarithm); the mean and the signed rules are not.

**Corollary B.1 (nested documents).** Let a relevance score be $s(q, d) = \Psi_j A(Z_j(d, q))$ with the document as context and the query as option, a static encoder, a monotone $A$ and a nondecreasing $\Psi$. If $\mathrm{Bag}(d_a) \subseteq \mathrm{Bag}(d_b)$, then $s(q, d_a) \le s(q, d_b)$ for every query, so no query can rank $d_a$ strictly above $d_b$. A NevIR instance is correct only if each of its two queries ranks its own document strictly first; one of these two strict rankings puts the contained document above the containing one, so every instance whose documents are nested as bags (equal bags included) is failed by every such scorer, for every parameter value.

**Scope-tracking masks.** A scenario has arms O, P, N and S, an option $o^{y}$ correct in O, P and S, an option $o^{1-y}$ correct in N, and a one-bit mask. For a relation $R \subseteq V \times V$ (token $v$ supports option token $w$), the scenario is $R$-separable if (i) every arm contains a token that supports some token of $o^{y}$; (ii) no token of any arm supports a token of $o^{1-y}$; and (iii) in N every supporting token lies inside the mask, and in O, P and S none does.

**Proposition C (possibility).** Let $c_{ij} = 1$ if $(x_i, y_j) \in R$ and 0 otherwise, and $\beta > 0$. The signed maximum of Eq. ({{ref:eq:signed}}) predicts $o^{y}$ for O, P and S and $o^{1-y}$ for N in every $R$-separable scenario.

**Proof.** By (ii) every pair score of $o^{1-y}$ is 0, so $S(o^{1-y}) = 0$ in every arm. Let $J$ be the nonempty set of tokens of $o^{y}$ supported in the arm. In O, P and S, for $j \in J$ the maximum over unmasked tokens is 1 and over masked tokens 0 by (iii), so $a_j = 1$, and $a_j = 0$ for $j \notin J$; hence $S(o^{y}) = |J|/m > 0$. In N the unmasked maximum is 0 and the masked maximum 1 for $j \in J$, so $a_j = -\beta$ and $S(o^{y}) = -\beta|J|/m < 0$. $\square$

The scorer of Proposition C is itself symmetric over (state, mask) pairs; it separates the arms only because its mask carries positional information (Corollary C.1). Proposition C is an existence statement for a chosen $R$; whether trained pair scores represent such a relation and whether a resolver's masks satisfy (iii) are empirical questions, which D1 and A4 measure.

## 3.6 Contextual encoders and released systems

No exact statement is made for contextual encoders: their token states depend on the whole sequence, so permuted or extended contexts generally change the states and the premises of Propositions A and B fail. Every released system of Section 5 and every fine-tuned model of Section 6 is contextual; for them the diagnostics measure behavior, not a theorem. Cross-encoders, monoT5 and language-model judges read query and document jointly and lie outside the setting; they serve as references for what the items permit.

# 4. Diagnostics and data

## 4.1 NegPairs-Perm

**Design.** A scenario has a short context, a question asking what someone should do, two action options, and four arms that share question and options: an affirmative context O, a paraphrase P without negation, a negation of the decisive words N (after which the other option is correct), and a negation of an irrelevant detail S (after which the answer stays that of O). In NegPairs-Perm the context is one sentence of two coordinated clauses, a decisive clause and another clause with a detail that does not matter for the choice; each clause contains one edit site of the same kind (the same word $W$, or an insertion point after the same kind of auxiliary). N applies one negation edit at the site in the decisive clause and S the same edit at the site in the other clause, so N and S contain exactly the same words and differ only in where the edit sits (Table {{ref:tab:exampleperm}}, Appendix A). One pattern per cue family: insertion of *not* after an auxiliary or copula (syntactic); replacement of the same determiner *a*, *an* or *some* by *no* (quantifier); replacement of the same adjective or participle by a prefixed form *un-*, *in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *ir-*, *dis-* or *non-* (affixal); and the pairs *has/lacks*, *have/lack*, *had/lacked*, *with/without*, *present/absent* (lexical). Clause order (decisive clause first or second) was balanced by a seeded shuffle, and acceptance was capped per family and order at «build.perm.cap_per_stratum».

**Construction.** Candidates were generated by `claude-sonnet-5` from a frozen prompt that returned the question, the options, the answer, the context with both edit sites marked, the connector, the decisive words, the negated form and the paraphrase; the arms were assembled deterministically from the marked context and passed eleven deterministic checks (among them word edit distance exactly 1 from O to N and to S, and equal token bags of N and S under two tokenizers), a near-duplicate check, blind verification of each arm by `claude-haiku-4-5-20251001` and, as an acceptance condition, by `Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct` [@yang2024qwen] in both option orders (Section 4.3). «data.perm.scenarios» scenarios («data.perm.items» items) were accepted from «build.perm.candidates» candidates before the budget cap: the seen families (syntactic, quantifier) reached their caps in both clause orders, the lexical family «build.perm.accepted.lexical.first» and «build.perm.accepted.lexical.second», the affixal family only «build.perm.accepted.affixal.first» and «build.perm.accepted.affixal.second», because mechanically prefixed forms were often rejected (Appendix A). $\mathrm{Bag}(N) = \mathrm{Bag}(S)$ holds under the tokenizers of all seven encoders of Section 7 in every scenario. Contexts have «data.perm.O_words.min» to «data.perm.O_words.max» words; cues are *not* («data.perm.cue.syntactic.not» scenarios), *no* («data.perm.cue.quantifier.no»), *lacks* («data.perm.cue.lexical.lacks») and «data.perm.cue.affixal.n_distinct» affixal forms. Permutation pairs repeat a word across two clauses by design, so some arms read oddly, which the audit and the human check quantify (two further artifacts are recorded in Appendix A).

**Measures.** For a scenario and an arm $X \in \{P, S, N\}$, a decision change means that the predicted option for $X$ differs from that for O; over a set of scenarios, $\mathrm{flip}_{O\to X}$ is the percentage of decision changes and scope sensitivity is $\mathrm{SS} = \mathrm{flip}_{O\to N} - \mathrm{flip}_{O\to S}$, in points: 100 for a scorer that changes its decision for every N and no S context, 0 for one that reacts to N and S alike. On permutation pairs the order-balanced estimand

$$\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm,bal}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm,first}} + \mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm,second}}\right) \qquad ({{def:eq:ssbal}})$$

averages the scope sensitivities of the scenarios whose decisive clause comes first and second, so that a rule that reacts only to the clause in which a cue sits scores near zero whatever the shares of the two orders. Joint (N, S) accuracy is the percentage of scenarios in which both arms are answered correctly. Intervals are scenario-cluster percentile bootstrap intervals («cfg.boot_n» replicates; within clause order for the order-balanced estimand), instance-level for NevIR (Appendix B.3).

## 4.2 Standard minimal pairs: NegPairs v1 and NegPairs-Fresh

NegPairs v1 («data.v1.scenarios» scenarios) and NegPairs-Fresh («data.fresh.scenarios» scenarios, built after the manifest of Section 7 was frozen) are standard minimal pairs of the same four-arm form, in which the S cue negates an irrelevant detail with words that differ from those of N, so that a static scorer may separate them through token identity and scope sensitivity mixes word identity with position; permutation pairs remove the identity component by construction. Syntactic and quantifier cues occur in every split of v1; affixal and lexical cues occur only in its test split and in Fresh, so that "unseen family" means a cue family absent from all training and selection data. The v1 train («data.v1.train.scenarios» scenarios) and dev («data.v1.dev.scenarios») splits are the only training and selection data used in this article (Sections 6 and 7). Construction details, filters and the rejection funnels are in Appendix A; Table {{ref:tab:data}} lists scenario counts by split and family.

## 4.3 Verification, audit and human check

Each arm of every candidate was shown alone to the first verifier, which had to return the expected option and judge the text natural; the second verifier, from another model family, saw each arm in both option orders, its verdict read from float32 letter logits, and an arm passed only if the same option won in both orders. A seeded 10% sample of each set was re-verified by `claude-opus-5`: on NegPairs-Perm the answers agreed in «build.audit.perm.answers_agree» of «build.audit.perm.answers_total» arm judgments, but the auditor judged many permutation arms unnatural (all four arms natural in only «build.audit.perm.agree_all» of «build.audit.perm.n» scenarios); on NegPairs-Fresh «build.audit.fresh.agree_all» of «build.audit.fresh.n» scenarios agreed on all four arms. The second verifier read about a third of v1's scenarios differently (mostly on N), so the sets it filtered may be easier for language models than v1 (Appendix A).

## 4.4 NevIR and the nested subset

NevIR [@weller2024] is a retrieval benchmark of «nevir.instances» test instances (dataset revision `«nevir.revision_short»`), each with two documents that differ by a negation (CondaQA passages and their scope or affirmative edits) and two crowd-written queries, $q_1$ relevant to $d_1$ and $q_2$ to $d_2$. An instance is correct if $s(q_1, d_1) > s(q_1, d_2)$ and $s(q_2, d_2) > s(q_2, d_1)$ (paired accuracy in percent; ties are errors; random ranking gives «nevir.published.random»). The nested subset consists of the instances with $\mathrm{Bag}(d_1) \subseteq \mathrm{Bag}(d_2)$ or $\mathrm{Bag}(d_2) \subseteq \mathrm{Bag}(d_1)$ under a word tokenizer: «nevir.nested» instances («nevir.nested_pct|%»), of which «nevir.equal_bags» have equal bags (documents that are permutations of each other). Corollary B.1 says that every static monotone MaxSim scorer fails all of them; for contextual systems the subset measures how much of a negation that only inserts or reorders words reaches the score.

# 5. Released systems

## 5.1 Systems and protocol

We scored «pub.n_checkpoints» public checkpoints exactly as released, through their own tokenization, length limits and similarity, each as a function $s(\text{query}, \text{document})$ (identifiers, revisions and settings in Table {{ref:tab:pubsys}}, Appendix B.6). Late interaction: ColBERTv2 [@santhanam2022] with its native scoring, answerai-colbert-small-v1 [@answerai2024], GTE-ModernColBERT-v1 [@lighton2025; @warner2024], a PyLate checkpoint [@chaffin2025] whose encoding we replicated without the library, and the dense, sparse and multi-vector heads of bge-m3 [@chen2024bge], computed from the released weights and checked against the reference implementation (Appendix B.6). Dense bi-encoders: bge-base-en-v1.5 [@xiao2023], e5-base-v2 [@wang2022e5], all-mpnet-base-v2 [@reimers2019; @song2020] and gte-modernbert-base [@alibaba2025], with their documented prefixes. Learned sparse: SPLADE cocondenser-ensembledistil [@formal2022splade]. Cross-encoders: ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2 [@stce], bge-reranker-base and bge-reranker-v2-m3 [@baai_reranker], mxbai-rerank-base-v1 [@shakir2024]; and monoT5 [@nogueira2020] base and 3B. Pointwise judges: Phi-4-mini-instruct [@abouelenin2025], OLMo-2-7B-Instruct [@olmo2024] and Qwen3-8B [@yang2025qwen3], each asked whether the proposed answer is correct according to the context (or whether a document is relevant to a query), scored by $\log P(\mathrm{Yes}) - \log P(\mathrm{No})$ at the first generated token; the models that built or verified NegPairs are not among the judges, and Qwen3-8B, from the family of the second verifier, is flagged as such. On NegPairs the query is the answer option and the document the context followed by the question, for every scorer in this article; the judges see the three fields separately. Encoders ran in float32 (bge-m3 in float16, as its reference); monoT5 and the judges ran in bfloat16, with the two or three logits that decide a score computed in float32 from the state entering the output head: a first pass with bfloat16 logits had tied «pub.prec.ties_prev_total» NevIR comparisons across the five affected systems, and the float32 head removed every tie and raised monoT5-3B's paired accuracy by «pub.prec.monot5l.nevir_all.delta» points (Appendix B.6).

# 7. Trained heads on frozen states: a prespecified study

## 7.1 Design

The study was prespecified in a manifest (version 3.0, frozen 5 October 2026, SHA-256 `«lock.sha_short»…`) that fixed the propositions, the data design, the estimands, a gate and the rules with their thresholds before any new data set was generated and before any model was trained under it. Two earlier manifests had tested the module on CondaQA and on the first NegPairs test split; the present rules were written after those outcomes were known, which is why they use NegPairs-Fresh, built after the freeze, and why every outcome is reported (Appendix B.4).

Seven frozen encoders supplied the token states: two static (E0, a hash encoder with a fixed random unit vector per token type; E3, GloVe [@pennington2014]) and five contextual (E1 MiniLM [@wang2020minilm], E2 DeBERTa-v3-base [@he2021; @he2023], E4 MPNet [@song2020], E5 BGE [@xiao2023], E6 ColBERTv2's token embeddings [@santhanam2022]; Appendix B.1). Eight trained conditions (A1 to A6, D1, M1), the zero-shot M0, the controls C0 (head-only, a linear function of the mean states) and C2 (option-only, context-blind), and a fine-tuned reference C1 (DeBERTa-v3-base as a multiple-choice cross-encoder) were trained on v1 train with selection on v1 dev, ten seeds, the learning rate per encoder chosen on dev in a pilot; «train.n_runs» runs in all (Appendix B.2). Rules were evaluated per encoder on seed means with thresholds fixed in the manifest (Table {{ref:tab:rules}}): a responsiveness gate G (A1 seed-mean SS on v1 dev of at least «thr.gate_SS_seen_dev» points, computed before any test metric and fixing the number $k$ of evaluated encoders), R2 (gain of A4 over A1 in SS on the unseen families of Fresh), R3 (gain of A6 over A4 in $\mathrm{flip}_{O\to S}$ on the Fresh scenarios whose S cue shares a sentence with a decisive word), R8 (A1 seed-mean $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm,bal}}$ on the seen families of Perm, contextual encoders only), and three exact rules for the static encoders in float64 with a tolerance of «thr.exact_tolerance»: R5 (no score decrease on any insertion pair, Proposition B), R6 (identical scores for N and S under every unmasked rule, Proposition A) and R7 (zero paired accuracy on the nested NevIR subset, Corollary B.1). A statistical rule is met if the seed mean reaches its threshold, the quantity is positive in at least «thr.min_positive_seeds» of «n.seeds» seeds, and both the seed-level $t$ interval and the scenario-cluster bootstrap interval at level $1 - 0.05/k$ lie above zero (Appendix B.3).

## 7.2 Exact checks and rule outcomes

The implementation reproduced Proposition B on the «R5.pairs_total_per_encoder» insertion pairs of the three test sets («R5.checks» checks over E0 and E3 with A1, A3, M1 in ten seeds and M0; «R5.violations_total» violations among «R5.n_scores_total» option scores), Proposition A on the «R6.pairs» permutation pairs («R6.checks» checks, «R6.score_violations_total» violations; every unmasked rule of E0 and E3 therefore has $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm}} = 0$ and joint accuracy 0 in every seed), and Corollary B.1 on the «R7.nested_instances» nested NevIR instances («R7.checks» checks, «R7.violations_total» violations). All static-encoder values on Perm reported here are float64 (Appendix B.5).

Six of seven encoders passed the gate (E3 failed with «G.E3.mean» points). R2 was met only for the hash encoder: A4 minus A1 unseen-family SS on Fresh was «R2.E0.mean|+» points for E0 («ci:R2.E0»; positive in «R2.E0.pos» of «R2.E0.n» seeds), «R2.E6.mean|+» for E6 (both intervals above zero, below the threshold), «R2.E2.mean|+» for E2 (seed interval includes zero) and at most «R2.E5.mean|+» for E5, E4 and E1 (with static states A4's gain reflects the supplied mask alone). R3 was met only for E2 («R3.E2.mean|+» points; «ci:R3.E2»). R8 was met for three of five contextual encoders: A1's order-balanced $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm}}$ on the seen families was «R8.E4.mean» points with E4 («ci:R8.E4»), «R8.E6.mean» with E6 and «R8.E5.mean» with E5, against «R8.E1.mean» with E1 (both intervals above zero but below the threshold) and «R8.E2.mean» with E2 (Table {{ref:tab:perm}}); the fine-tuned cross-encoder C1 reached «sm.perm.C1.C1.seen.SS_bal» points.


## 7.3 Supplied scope, probes and clause order

On the permutation pairs, where every unmasked rule over static states is at exactly zero, the signed maximum with dataset scope (D1) reached an order-balanced $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm}}$ of «f64.E3.D1.seen.SS_bal» points with GloVe and «f64.E0.D1.seen.SS_bal» with hash states (joint accuracy «f64.E3.D1.seen.pooled.joint_NS_acc|%» and «f64.E0.D1.seen.pooled.joint_NS_acc|%»), resolver scope (A4) «f64.E3.A4.seen.SS_bal» and «f64.E0.A4.seen.SS_bal», and the sentence-wide mask (A6), which marks the same multiset in N and S, stayed at «f64.E3.A6.seen.SS_bal» and «f64.E0.A6.seen.SS_bal». A position-aware mask therefore separates permutation pairs over static states, as Corollary C.1 permits; the trained pair scores realized part of it, far less with hash states than with GloVe. With the contextual encoders D1 reached «rng.perm.D1.seen_SS_bal.contextual.min» to «rng.perm.D1.seen_SS_bal.contextual.max» points and A4 «rng.perm.A4.seen_SS_bal.contextual.min» to «rng.perm.A4.seen_SS_bal.contextual.max». On standard pairs the static encoders separated N from S only where the cue words had been seen (E0 «sm.fresh.E0.A1.seen.SS» points on the seen families of Fresh, «R1.E0.mean» on the unseen), zero-shot MaxSim stayed between «rng.fresh.M0.SS.allsets.min» and «rng.fresh.M0.SS.allsets.max» points on every set, and the learned gate A5 changed A1's unseen-family SS by «rng.fresh.A5mA1.unseen.all7.min» to «rng.fresh.A5mA1.unseen.all7.max» points, far from what supplied scope gave D1.

Do the frozen states encode on which clause a negation edit sits? For every N and S arm of NegPairs-Perm, every word of the two clauses except the cue and the unedited occurrence of $W$ was labeled by whether it lies in the clause that carries the edit (each word occurs once with each label), and an L2-regularized logistic regression [@pedregosa2011] on the token state was evaluated by scenario-grouped cross-validation on the seen families («probe.E1.n_words_seen» words). For E0 and E3 the accuracy is exactly 50%, the pipeline check; for the contextual encoders it lay between «probe.E6.cv_mean|%» (E6) and «probe.E2.cv_mean|%» (E2). A neutral twin of each syntactic N and S arm, with *also* inserted instead of *not*, measures how much of this is proximity to an inserted word [@hewitt2019]: the selectivity (negation probe minus twin probe) was «probe.E1.selectivity» points for E1 but only «probe.E2.selectivity» for E2, and it does not track the rule outcome: E1 has the highest selectivity and did not meet R8, E4 («probe.E4.selectivity») the highest permutation scope sensitivity.

The strongest regularity in the permutation data is the asymmetry between clause orders. With the decisive clause first, A1's $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{perm}}$ on the seen families was «sm.perm.E4.A1.seen.first.SS» (E4), «sm.perm.E6.A1.seen.first.SS» (E6), «sm.perm.E1.A1.seen.first.SS» (E1), «sm.perm.E2.A1.seen.first.SS» (E2) and «sm.perm.E5.A1.seen.first.SS» (E5) points; with the decisive clause second it fell to «sm.perm.E4.A1.seen.second.SS», «sm.perm.E6.A1.seen.second.SS», «sm.perm.E1.A1.seen.second.SS», «sm.perm.E2.A1.seen.second.SS» and «sm.perm.E5.A1.seen.second.SS». The fine-tuned cross-encoder showed the same asymmetry in a stronger form («sm.perm.C1.C1.seen.first.SS» against «sm.perm.C1.C1.seen.second.SS» points), and so did the released rerankers of Section 5. A cue in the first clause changes decisions, a cue in the second clause much less, whether or not it negates the decisive words; nothing in standard minimal pairs, where the N cue always sits on the decisive words, teaches a model that a negation elsewhere should leave the answer unchanged.

# 8. Discussion

**What the exact limits say and do not say.** Over static token states the propositions leave one way to react to negation: through the pair scores of the cue and the edited words themselves, wherever they stand. The exact rules confirmed this without exception, and static scorers separated N from S only where cue and edited words differed and had been seen in training.

**What released systems do.** The permutation pairs were designed so that only scope, not the presence of a cue, can separate N from S. On them the released late-interaction systems behaved as if their token states were static: their scope sensitivity is indistinguishable from zero, and their N accuracy equals their O accuracy. Dense bi-encoders, SPLADE and the weaker cross-encoders were not better, the strongest rerankers reached «pub.monot5l.perm.SS_bal» (monoT5-3B) and «pub.mxbai.perm.SS_bal» (mxbai) points, and small language models reading the same items as a question reached «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.min» to «pub.rng.llm.SS_bal.max»: the items are not beyond reach, and what the retrieval scorers lack is not a representation of negation but a representation of where it applies that survives their scoring. On the human-written NevIR data the same ordering holds with smaller gaps, and on documents that are permutations of each other a learned sparse representation behaves almost exactly like a bag of words.

**Why fine-tuning on minimal pairs does not fix it.** Standard minimal pairs can be separated by the presence of a negation word near the decisive words, and that is what the fine-tuned models learned: their decision changes rose under N and under S alike, their scope sensitivity on Fresh rose far more than on Perm, and their standard-retrieval quality fell. The trained heads of Section 7 tell the same story from the other side: frozen contextual states contain clause information that a linear probe reads (partly as proximity to an inserted word), a maximum trained on standard pairs uses a fraction of it, mostly when the decisive clause comes first, and a mask built from the decision separates the pairs where no unmasked rule can. For token-pair scorers that must respect negation, these results argue for designs in which position reaches the pair scores, and for training data in which irrelevant negations are frequent.

# 9. Limitations

NegPairs is model-generated and model-verified, by one model family as generator, first verifier and auditor and a second family as second verifier; the acceptance condition removes scenarios on which the two disagree, so the sets may be easier for language models than v1. The human check covers all permutation scenarios but only a sample of Fresh. The audit judged many permutation arms unnatural, the Perm contexts carry a capitalization artifact of the generator prompt, the affixal family has «build.perm.affixal_total» scenarios and the lexical family a single cue. The released systems were used with their documented settings and one untuned prompt per judge, and a different formulation could move the judges' values; Qwen3-8B belongs to the family of the second verifier. The fine-tuning experiment used one recipe, two models and three seeds, trained on standard pairs. The prespecified study was revised twice after earlier results were known, its prespecification is self-attested, its thresholds are judgment values without a power analysis, two outcomes fell within one point of a threshold, and its scorers were trained on «data.v1.train.scenarios» scenarios whose arms the second verifier reads differently in about «n.qwen_train_disagree_pct|%» of cases. All results concern short English texts of a fixed template and one retrieval benchmark.

# 10. Conclusion

A maximum over token-pair scores from static token states sees a context as a bag of tokens and can only raise its scores when tokens are added; permutation minimal pairs and nested document pairs make these limits visible on concrete items. Released late-interaction, dense and sparse retrievers behave on those items as the static limit predicts, rerankers only somewhat better, while small language-model judges separate them, and fine-tuning on standard minimal pairs teaches the presence of negation, not its scope. Negation scope has to enter a token-pair scorer through contextual states that keep it in a form the maximum can use, which the frozen encoders did only in part, or through a position-aware mask, which separated the pairs where nothing else could.

E0 (hash) split the text into word and punctuation tokens and mapped each lower-cased token type to a fixed unit vector of dimension «enc.E0.dim» drawn from a Gaussian seeded with a SHA-256 hash of the token. E1 `sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2`, E2 `microsoft/deberta-v3-base`, E4 `sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2`, E5 `BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5` and E6 `colbert-ir/colbertv2.0` (BERT-base followed by the checkpoint's projection to «enc.E6.dim» dimensions, document mode) supplied last-layer token states with special tokens removed, each state L2-normalized, a maximum length of «cfg.max_length_negpairs» tokens for NegPairs and «cfg.max_length_nevir» for NevIR (no text truncated), inference in bfloat16; revisions E1 `«enc.E1.revision_short»…`, E2 `«enc.E2.revision_short»…`, E4 `«enc.E4.revision_short»…`, E5 `«enc.E5.revision_short»…`, E6 `«enc.E6.revision_short»…`. For E0 and E3 the exact rules and every Perm and NevIR metric were computed from float64 scores on the CPU.

## B.2 Pair scorer, training and controls

The pair scorer is $c_{ij} = e_{ij} + \lambda \tanh(a_{ij})$ with $e_{ij} = F(h_i)^{\top}F(u_j)/\sqrt{«cfg.proj»}$, $F$ a two-layer perceptron ($d \to «cfg.proj» \to «cfg.proj»$), $a_{ij}$ an affinity network on $[h_i; u_j; h_i - u_j; h_i \odot u_j]$ (layers $4d \to «cfg.hidden» \to «cfg.hidden4» \to 1$, ReLU, dropout «cfg.dropout»), $\lambda = \sigma(\theta)$; A3 uses $\tau = \mathrm{softplus}(t) + «code.tau_eps»$. Context and question were encoded jointly as one text and each option separately; for NevIR the document takes the role of the context and the query that of the option. Each condition was trained per encoder and seed with AdamW [@loshchilov2019] (weight decay «cfg.weight_decay»), batches of «cfg.batch» items, at most «cfg.epochs» epochs with early stopping on dev accuracy (patience «cfg.patience»), gradient clipping at «cfg.grad_clip», selection of the checkpoint with the highest dev accuracy, bfloat16 autocast with the loss in float32; learning rate per encoder from the grid «lr.grid.1», «lr.grid.2», «lr.grid.3» by mean dev accuracy of A1 over three seeds in the pilot («lr.n_3e3» encoders received «lr.grid.3», E5 and E6 «lr.grid.2»); seeds «seed.1», «seed.2», «seed.3», «seed.4», «seed.5», «seed.6», «seed.7», «seed.8», «seed.9», «seed.10». Parameter counts are in Table {{ref:tab:params}}. The scope resolver of A4 and A6 combined a cue lexicon with scope rules over a spaCy dependency parse [@honnibal2020] (model `«res.spacy»`): a clause-level negator scoped over the subtree of its predicate without the subject, a negative determiner or pronoun over its nearest verbal ancestor, a lexical or affixal cue over the phrase it headed; it detected the declared cue in «res.fresh.unseen.N.cue_detected.pct|%» of the unseen-family N items of Fresh, and on Perm its scope covered the decisive words in «res.perm.unseen.N.decisive_in_scope.pct|%» to «res.perm.seen.N.decisive_in_scope.pct|%» of the N arms. C0 scored each option with a linear function of the mean context state, the mean option state, their product and absolute difference; C2 with a two-layer perceptron of the option's mean state; C1 was `microsoft/deberta-v3-base` as a multiple-choice cross-encoder (learning rate «cfg.c1_lr», «cfg.c1_epochs» epochs, maximum length «cfg.c1_max_length», selection on dev accuracy, ten seeds).

## B.3 Uncertainty

Seed-level $t$ intervals (ten seeds, nine degrees of freedom) were paired across conditions within a seed for contrasts; the rules used the level $1 - 0.05/k$ («stat.conf_R2R3|%» for R2 and R3 with $k$ = «G.k_R2_R3», «stat.conf_R8|%» for R8 with $k$ = «G.k_R8»), and 95% elsewhere. Scenario-cluster percentile bootstrap intervals [@efron1993] («cfg.boot_n» replicates; all arms and seeds of a scenario resampled together, within clause order for the order-balanced estimand) were computed alongside, and NevIR accuracies received instance-level bootstrap intervals; for the released systems and the fine-tuned models the same bootstrap was applied to single runs. Intervals that include zero are read as inconclusive, not as equivalence; family-wise error control was applied only within each rule across encoders.

## B.4 Design history, pilot and provenance

Manifest v1.1 tested the module on CondaQA [@ravichander2022] with the answer strings *yes*, *no* and *don't know* as candidates; no frozen-encoder condition exceeded the majority-label rate (bare answer strings give a pair scorer no lexical anchor in the context). Manifest v2.0 (frozen 4 October 2026) moved the test to NegPairs v1 with three encoders, five seeds, a gate on the test split and rules R1 to R3 and R5; the gate was passed by E1 only, R2 was positive in all five seeds but below its threshold, R3 was not met, R5 was met with zero violations. Version 3.0 was written after these results: permutation pairs and the exact rules R6 and R7 test the propositions directly; seven encoders, ten seeds, a learning rate per encoder chosen on dev, a dev gate and a second-verifier acceptance condition replace the v2.0 design; the statistical rules use NegPairs-Fresh; M0, M1 and ColBERTv2 extend the module to the family; NevIR and its nested subset are added; R1 became descriptive. The manifest's hash was recorded by the build notebook before the first API call and by the experiment notebook before the first training step; the pilot (before any test file was parsed) ran a data audit, encoder checks, exact checks on dev, a two-epoch C1 check and the learning-rate grid. The run (package `cacsa2` «env.cacsa2»; PyTorch [@paszke2019] and Transformers [@wolf2020]) executed in one Colab session on an «env.gpu» GPU («train.n_grid_runs» grid runs, 140 control runs, 10 C1 runs, seven M0 scorings, one M0-native scoring; «train.time_total_h» hours of training). Every file of every run directory is listed with its SHA-256 value; a second script that does not import the package recomputed every per-run metric, the gate quantity and the rule intervals from the raw predictions («indep.compared» values, «indep.mismatches» mismatches); the float64 follow-up for E0 and E3 changed «f64.n_seed_mean_differences» seed means of masked rules by at most «f64.max_abs_seed_mean_diff» points.

## B.5 Rounding

The statements of Section 3 hold in exact arithmetic. For the static encoders the exact rules and every Perm and NevIR value were computed in float64 with a tolerance of «thr.exact_tolerance» (a margin of «cfg.nevir_margin» for NevIR rankings); on the GPU the bfloat16 decisions differed from the float64 decisions in «R6.E0.gpu_vs_f64_disagreements» (E0) and «R6.E3.gpu_vs_f64_disagreements» (E3) of the «R6.n_item_predictions_per_encoder» N and S predictions, always at near-ties; for every other system the scores are float32 or bfloat16 as stated, and ties count as errors in NevIR and as the first option in NegPairs. Percentages and points are rounded half up to two decimals; differences and ranges are computed from unrounded values.

## B.6 Released systems and fine-tuning

Released systems were scored on an «pub.env.gpu» GPU (Transformers «pub.env.transformers», sentence-transformers «pub.env.sentence_transformers», PyTorch «pub.env.torch»), each with the settings recorded in its score file (Table {{ref:tab:pubsys}}); the three heads of bge-m3 were re-implemented from the released weights and agreed with the reference implementation on «pub.check.bgem3.pairs» pairs to within «pub.check.bgem3.maxdiff»; the judges' prompts, the answer-token sets and the document truncation at «pub.llm.max_doc_tokens» tokens are in the released code. The bfloat16 first pass had tied «pub.prec.monot5l.prev.nevir_ties» (monoT5-3B), «pub.prec.monot5b.prev.nevir_ties» (monoT5-base), «pub.prec.qwen3.prev.nevir_ties» (Qwen3-8B), «pub.prec.phi.prev.nevir_ties» (Phi-4-mini) and «pub.prec.olmo.prev.nevir_ties» (OLMo-2) NevIR comparisons; its NevIR paired accuracies were «pub.prec.monot5l.prev.nevir_all», «pub.prec.monot5b.prev.nevir_all», «pub.prec.qwen3.prev.nevir_all», «pub.prec.phi.prev.nevir_all» and «pub.prec.olmo.prev.nevir_all» against «pub.prec.monot5l.new.nevir_all», «pub.prec.monot5b.new.nevir_all», «pub.prec.qwen3.new.nevir_all», «pub.prec.phi.new.nevir_all» and «pub.prec.olmo.new.nevir_all» with the float32 decisive logits reported in this article. Cross-encoders return their raw relevance logit; monoT5 the log-probability of *true* in the softmax over *false* and *true* at the first decoder step; bge-m3's sparse score is the sum over shared token ids of the products of the relu weights, its multi-vector score the mean over query vectors of the maximum cosine. The fine-tuning of Section 6 trained the encoder and the projection with AdamW (weight decay «ft.cfg.weight_decay», gradient clipping «ft.cfg.grad_clip», warm-up fraction «ft.cfg.warmup», seeds «ft.cfg.seeds»); selected epochs were «ft.colbertv2.np.epochs» (ColBERTv2, NegPairs), «ft.answerai.np.epochs» (answerai, NegPairs), «ft.colbertv2.nv.epochs» and «ft.answerai.nv.epochs» (NevIR); SciFact came from the BEIR distribution (zip SHA-256 `«ft.scifact.sha_short»…`, «ft.scifact.n_qrels» relevance judgments), with each system's own document length limit. Scoring a trained model took the same code path as the released checkpoint. The ColBERTv2 scores of Section 5 agreed with those of Section 7's bfloat16 run (M0-native) in «pub.anchor.perm.agree|%» of the Perm decisions and «pub.anchor.nevir.agree|%» of the NevIR comparisons.
